# Vision

Telepath is the answer to a simple question: what would a personal messaging service look
like if it worked for YOU — not for an advertiser, not for a platform's engagement graph,
and not behind an app store's approval queue?

It is your WhatsApp, mirrored live: every conversation, real names, photos, unread badges,
new messages landing while you watch. And it is more than a mirror — it carries an analyst.
One tap reads a whole conversation and tells you what is actually going on in it: who each
person is *as their messages show them*, who leads and who goes quiet, how fast people
answer, when the room is alive, and — the part nobody else will build — how YOU sound in it.
The next tap doesn't re-read history; it reads only what happened since, updates the
picture, and leads with what changed. A third tap drafts your next reply in your own
register, down to the emoji you measurably use.

No gatekept store would approve this app. The provider's terms forbid the link; the
analysis profiles people who never signed up for anything. Telepath ships anyway — openly —
because the user owns the runtime, and it answers every objection in the open: the ToS risk
is stated on the surface that matters, third parties are pseudonymised before any model
turn (the model sees the conversation's shape, never its roster), photos never leave the
machine, drafts are never auto-sent, and the whole derived record lives in the user's own
file, deletable in one act.

The demonstration underneath is the Snug Protocol's whole pitch in one app: a linked-device
session in a local helper the shell supervises (ADR-0032), reached only through a governed
transport; a host that pushes a doorbell into a sandboxed iframe without widening it by one
capability (ADR-0034); an analysis whose privacy boundary is enforced by tested code rather
than policy prose; and an app that raises the benchmark for what "a messaging client" can
mean when the user, not the platform, holds the keys.
